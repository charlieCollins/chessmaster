# Chunking and templates — why pattern volume matters

**Key sources**
- Chase, W. G., & Simon, H. A. (1973). Perception in chess. *Cognitive Psychology, 4*(1), 55–81.
- Gobet, F., & Simon, H. A. (1996). Templates in chess memory: A mechanism for recalling
  several boards. *Cognitive Psychology, 31*(1), 1–40.
- Overview (open access): Gobet et al., PMC4361603 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4361603
  (not downloaded: the host timed out; see `corpus/SOURCES.md`).

## Findings
- **Recall experiments:** after a few seconds' look, masters reconstruct *game*
  positions far better than weaker players, but the advantage largely disappears for
  *randomly* placed pieces (Chase & Simon). Strength is knowledge of meaningful
  configurations, not a better general memory.
- **Chunks:** strong players perceive the board as familiar groups of pieces
  ("chunks"). Chase & Simon's rough estimate of a master's chunk library is in the
  tens of thousands.
- **Templates** (Gobet & Simon): frequently seen chunks grow into larger, partly
  flexible structures with "slots", linked to plans and typical moves. That's why a
  master *sees* candidate moves instantly in familiar structures.

## Strength of evidence
Recall and perception findings have been replicated many times. How exactly chunks
turn into good *moves* is still modelled rather than proven.

## What it means here
- **Volume of real positions** builds the library: theme-grouped puzzles from real
  games (Lichess) are ideal raw material; random or artificial positions teach less.
- **Group by pattern** (a knight fork on f7, a back-rank mate, Lucena) so related
  positions are seen together, then mixed later.
- Short, many reps beat a few long sessions of reading.
